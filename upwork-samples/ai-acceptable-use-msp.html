1. Purpose and scope

Kestwick is a managed service provider with 14 staff supporting about 45 client organizations, mostly dental and medical practices, accounting firms and small manufacturers. Our technicians hold administrator access to client systems through our remote monitoring and management (RMM) platform. A mistake made with that access, or a password leaked from it, can reach many clients at once. This policy sets out how staff may use artificial intelligence (AI) tools so that AI makes us faster without putting that access, or our clients' data, at risk.

It applies to every employee and contractor, and it covers:

  • stand-alone AI tools, such as chat assistants and coding assistants;
  • AI features built into tools we already use, such as ticket summaries in the professional services automation (PSA) platform and script suggestions in the RMM platform;
  • any AI output that goes into client systems, scripts, reports or messages.

Where a client contract is stricter than this policy, the contract wins. Several clients are covered by HIPAA or the FTC Safeguards Rule, and their agreements limit where their data may go.

4. Rules for technical work

4.1 Scripts and commands written with AI

  • Read and understand every line before running it. If you cannot explain what a line does, do not run it.
  • Test on a single lab or non-production device first. Never deploy an AI-written script through the RMM platform to more than one device without testing.
  • A second technician reviews any AI-assisted script before it runs on a server, on more than one client, or on more than 10 devices. The service manager also approves any run on more than one client. The change ticket records that AI was used, who reviewed it, how to undo the change and how to check that it worked.
  • Check that every module, package or download an AI tool suggests exists on its official source and comes from the expected publisher. AI tools sometimes name packages that do not exist, and attackers register those names.

4.2 AI agents and automation

  • No AI tool is given standing administrator credentials, access to the password vault, or access to the client documentation platform.
  • AI features in the RMM and PSA platforms may suggest actions, but a technician approves each change before it runs. Settings that let an AI feature act on its own ("agent" or "autopilot" modes) stay off until the security lead and the owner approve them for a named use.

4.3 Treat outside content as untrusted

Emails, tickets, client files and web pages can contain hidden text written to instruct an AI tool. When an AI tool summarizes or reads such content, treat the result as information, not as an instruction. Never rely on an AI summary to decide that a reported email, link or attachment is safe; check it with our email security tools and the usual triage steps.

4.4 Check facts before acting on them

AI tools can state wrong answers with confidence. Before you change a security setting, firewall rule or backup job because an AI tool suggested it, confirm the setting in the vendor's own documentation. Facts and figures in anything sent to a client, such as a quarterly security report, are checked by the person who sends it.

5. Other uses

  • Client messages. An AI tool may help draft a reply. The sender reads and corrects it, and the rules in section 3 apply to anything pasted in. We do not use an AI chatbot to answer clients on our behalf.
  • Meeting notes. AI note-takers may be used in internal meetings only. They are not used on client calls, because those calls carry client data and the note-taker is not approved for it (Annex A).
  • Security awareness work. AI may help write phishing simulation emails only for a campaign the client has approved in writing. We never create a voice, image or video that imitates a real person.
  • Hiring and staff decisions. AI tools are not used to screen candidates or to make decisions about pay, performance or dismissal.
  • New AI features in existing tools. When a vendor adds an AI feature to a tool we or our clients use, it is treated as a new AI tool. Where the admin settings allow, it stays off in our tenant until reviewed. In client tenants, we tell the client and follow their decision.

6. Approving a new AI tool

Staff request a new tool with a PSA ticket that names the tool, the plan, what it will be used for and the most sensitive data it would need. Before deciding, the security lead checks and records:

  1. whether the vendor uses our inputs or outputs to train its models, on the specific plan we would buy;
  2. how long the vendor keeps inputs and outputs, and whether we can delete them;
  3. where the data is stored and processed;
  4. single sign-on with our identity provider and multi-factor authentication;
  5. admin controls and audit logs on that plan;
  6. which other companies the vendor passes data to;
  7. whether any client contract, including a business associate agreement, limits its use;
  8. the highest data class it may handle.

Free and personal plans are not approved for work, because their terms usually allow wider use of inputs. Browser extensions with AI features are not installed on work devices unless they are on the register.

7. Reporting problems

If something goes wrong

  • A secret was entered into any AI tool: treat it as exposed. Rotate the password, key or token at once, end its active sessions and check its logs for misuse, then tell the security lead. If it is a client's production credential, tell the client before rotating so the change does not cause an outage.
  • Client data went into a tool not approved for it: tell the security lead within one hour. Do not delete the conversation until the security lead has recorded it; it may be needed to find out what was shared. After that, delete it and ask the vendor to delete it where the plan allows.
  • An AI-assisted change caused an outage or a security problem: roll it back if it is safe to do so, and follow the incident response plan.

The security lead decides whether the client must be told, following the client's contract and the incident response plan. Reporting your own mistake promptly is never a disciplinary matter in itself; hiding one is.

8. Training, review and enforcement

  • Every new starter reads this policy and signs the acknowledgment before receiving access to client systems. All staff repeat the training each year and whenever the policy changes significantly.
  • The security lead reviews the AI tool register every six months and checks each vendor's current terms. The policy is reviewed at least once a year.
  • Exceptions are requested in a PSA ticket, approved in writing by the owner, and given an end date.
  • Breaking this policy may lead to loss of access to AI tools or client systems and to disciplinary action.
